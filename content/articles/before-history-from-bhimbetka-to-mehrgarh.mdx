<p><em>Series: History of Bharat, from the beginning to today · Part 01 · Era: Prehistory to c. 3300 BCE</em></p><blockquote><p><strong>In one line:</strong> Long before cities, kingdoms or writing, people in the Indian subcontinent were making tools, painting rock walls and learning to farm. This is where the story of Bharat begins.</p></blockquote><h2>The first footprints</h2><p>The story of Bharat does not begin with a king or a book. It begins with stones shaped by human hands. At <strong>Attirampakkam</strong>, near Chennai in Tamil Nadu, archaeologists have found hand-axes and cleavers of the Acheulian tradition that scientific dating places at no less than 1.07 million years, and possibly about <strong>1.5 million years</strong> old [6]. These were made by early human species, not by modern humans like us, but they show that the subcontinent was part of humanity's story from very early on.</p><p>The same site later showed something more surprising. A 2018 study found more refined, "Middle Palaeolithic" tools there dating back about <strong>385,000 years</strong>, which suggests that tool-making ideas were developing in India far earlier than many had assumed [7].</p><h2>Bhimbetka: the walls that remember</h2><p>In the foothills of the Vindhya hills in Madhya Pradesh, about 45 km from Bhopal, lie the <strong>rock shelters of Bhimbetka</strong>. The archaeologist <strong>V. S. Wakankar</strong> recognised their importance in 1957–58. Today more than 750 shelters are known, and some of them were lived in for over 100,000 years [1][4][5].</p><p>What makes Bhimbetka unforgettable is its art. On the sandstone walls, people painted animals, hunts, dances and group scenes in red and white. The oldest paintings are generally placed in the Mesolithic (Middle Stone Age); later layers continue into historical times. UNESCO inscribed the <strong>Rock Shelters of Bhimbetka</strong> as a World Heritage Site in <strong>2003</strong>, noting the unusually long continuity between the paintings and the culture of the villages around them today [1][2][3].</p><p>The name comes from local tradition linking the site to Bhima of the Mahabharata. That is a living story, not an archaeological finding, and it shows how places in India carry many layers of memory at once.</p><h2>Smaller tools, bigger changes</h2><p>After the last Ice Age ended, about 12,000 years ago, the climate became warmer. People in the subcontinent began making <strong>microliths</strong>, tiny sharp stone blades fitted into wooden or bone handles to make arrows, sickles and spears. Sites such as <strong>Bagor</strong> in Rajasthan and <strong>Adamgarh</strong> in Madhya Pradesh show communities that hunted and fished but also began to keep animals [10].</p><p>This period is called the <strong>Mesolithic</strong>, and it is the bridge between wandering hunters and settled farmers.</p><h2>Mehrgarh: learning to farm</h2><p>One of the most important early farming villages in South Asia is <strong>Mehrgarh</strong>, at the foot of the Bolan Pass in Balochistan (today in Pakistan). French archaeologists <strong>Jean-François Jarrige</strong> and <strong>Catherine Jarrige</strong> began excavating it in 1974. They dated its earliest farming levels to around <strong>7000 BCE</strong>, and most textbooks still give that date [10].</p><p>In 2025, a new study radiocarbon-dated tooth enamel from 23 burials in those early levels. It suggests that farming life at Mehrgarh began much later, around <strong>5200–4900 BCE</strong> [9]. Scholars are still debating the two timelines. Either way, Mehrgarh is one of the oldest known farming villages in South Asia.</p><p>At Mehrgarh, people grew <strong>barley and wheat</strong>, kept <strong>cattle, sheep and goats</strong>, lived in mud-brick houses, and buried their dead with beads and ornaments. Some people's molars were drilled with tiny flint tips, the oldest known evidence of drilling living human teeth [8]. Mehrgarh shows how village farming took root in this region and grew, step by step, into the life that later fed the Harappan cities.</p><h2>Many paths to settled life</h2><p>Farming did not begin in one place and spread in one line. Across the subcontinent, communities took different paths [10]:</p><ul><li><p><strong>Burzahom</strong> in Kashmir had people living in pit-houses dug into the ground.</p></li><li><p>In the <strong>Ganga plains</strong>, sites such as <strong>Lahuradewa</strong> and <strong>Koldihwa</strong> in Uttar Pradesh show early rice use.</p></li><li><p>In the <strong>Deccan and South India</strong>, Neolithic herders left large "ash mounds" from burning cattle dung, at sites such as <strong>Budihal</strong> and <strong>Sanganakallu</strong> in Karnataka.</p></li></ul><p>Later, in the <strong>Chalcolithic</strong> (copper–stone) age, communities in Rajasthan, Malwa and Maharashtra began using copper alongside stone, painting fine pottery and building larger villages.</p><h2>Why this matters today</h2><p>Prehistory can feel distant, but it is the foundation of everything that followed. The crops first grown in these villages, the habit of keeping cattle, the painted walls and the local knowledge of land and seasons became part of the deep inheritance of Bharat. The adivasi villages near Bhimbetka, whose art and life echo the old paintings, remind us that this past is not entirely past.</p><p><strong>A question to carry forward:</strong> if people were painting the walls of Bhimbetka thousands of years before any written record, how much of Bharat's story is still waiting in the ground?</p><h2>Key dates</h2><ul><li><p><strong>c. 1.07–1.5 million years ago:</strong> Acheulian tools made at Attirampakkam, Tamil Nadu</p></li><li><p><strong>c. 385,000 years ago:</strong> Middle Palaeolithic tools at Attirampakkam</p></li><li><p><strong>Over 100,000 years ago:</strong> Some Bhimbetka shelters already inhabited</p></li><li><p><strong>c. 10,000 BCE onward:</strong> Mesolithic microlith cultures; early Bhimbetka paintings</p></li><li><p><strong>c. 7000 BCE (older dating) or c. 5200 BCE (2025 dating):</strong> Earliest farming levels at Mehrgarh</p></li><li><p><strong>1957–58:</strong> V.
